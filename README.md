# Big Apple Sam

An After Dark–style night skyline you can zoom into. From far away it's just yellow window lights under a turning sky. Up close, every lit window is a small room with people living out their evenings.

Open `index.html` in a browser (or the GitHub Pages site). No build step and no dependencies.

## What's going on in there

- **One city per browser.** The skyline is generated once from a random seed and saved in `localStorage`, along with the clock and where you were looking. Come back tomorrow and it's the same city, and the clock has kept running while you were away.
- **Live clock.** By default the city runs at 1×, synced to your local time. Speeds go 60×, 600×, 6000× (a day in about four minutes) and 1 day/s (a year in about six minutes). "Back to now" re-syncs, and "Skip to 7 PM" jumps to the evening.
- **Years go by.** Fast-forward and the city ages. Every apartment has its own deterministic history: people grow older and go gray, kids grow up and leave at 18, students graduate, workers retire, couples move in together, hit rough patches, split up or have babies (who grow into kids with their own beds), pets come and go, and people eventually die. Households move out, the window goes dark with a FOR RENT sign, and new tenants arrive with their own furniture. Each apartment's window shows an "Over the years" timeline.
- **A real sky.** You're looking south from about 42°N. Stars (including Orion, the Pleiades, Cygnus, Vega, Sirius and friends) rise and set with sidereal time, and the moon moves and shows the correct phase.
- **Day and night.** The sun rises and sets on the real schedule for the date (tick "Always night" to keep it dark): twilight colors, a blue daytime sky, sunlit facades and glassy windows, with streetlights and headlights switching on at dusk.
- **About 6,000 residents.** Each has a job, a daily routine, two personality traits, a mood that shifts day to day, and a birthday. Many have a multi-week life arc: getting over a breakup, dating someone new, grieving a spouse, learning guitar, writing a novel, looking for work, training for a marathon, homesick, insomnia. Couples are newlyweds, steady, in a rough patch, or expecting (the baby arrives on a real date).
- **Street level.** Ground floors are a mix of businesses on their own hours. Restaurants, takeout counters, diners, pizza places, bars and nightclubs (10 PM to 4 AM, with colored lights and a crowded dance floor) run into the night. Cafés, bakeries, florists, bookstores, hardware stores, boutiques, barbers, pharmacies, dry cleaners and bank branches pull their grates down in the evening (the bank's ATM stays lit). Office towers have corporate lobbies with elevators and a security guard at the desk, busy with people in the daytime and dim at night. Wide buildings put a shop at one end and keep their lobby door at the other.
- **Massage parlors.** Two or three storefronts in every city are massage parlors, open from 6 PM to 3 AM, glowing red from the street with paper lanterns and a beaded curtain. Their regular is always Kevin, a grown-up who lives alone somewhere in the city. A couple of nights a week he heads out for a massage (his window says so), and if you catch it you can see him waiting on the bench before he disappears behind the curtain.
- **Office towers.** Each one has its own look (a plain grid, ribbon windows, a dark glass curtain wall, or an art-deco stone tower floodlit at the top), and some tall ones wear a lit crown that turns orange and purple for Halloween and red and green in December. Floors mix open-plan cubicles, corner offices, meeting rooms, kitchenettes and server rooms full of blinking LEDs. Each tenant's lights are a slightly different white. In the evening workers leave one by one so the windows go dark gradually; a few floors keep dim security lights on all night, and the occasional late worker sits under a desk lamp. From a distance they share the apartments' speckled texture, so the skyline reads as one city.
- **Hobbies at real-world rates.** Interests are assigned at roughly the rate you'd find them in a real city, so rare ones stay rare: about 10% play guitar, 3% make music on synths (a handful run modular rigs or grid sequencers), 3% shoot film (some develop it under a red safelight in the kitchen), 12% are particular about their coffee, 4% play retro games, 1% fly drones, and a theremin player turns up maybe once per city. About 8% of households keep an aquarium (some are shrimp tanks that glow blue at night), a third have windowsill plants, 7% own a stationary bike, and 8% go all-out for Halloween. There are a few professors grading lab reports and rewriting Specific Aims, families with a slide projector who hold slide nights, and the occasional parent making a video game with their kid.
- **Holidays, in season only.** Each household decorates on its own schedule. Halloween people put up red lights, a skeleton and a jack-o'-lantern sometime between mid-September and late October, and take them down in the first days of November. On Halloween night kids go trick-or-treating in costume with a parent, and there are more parties. Christmas decorations go up between the day after Thanksgiving and mid-December and come down between December 26 and early January. About 60% of households get a tree that glows in the dark room at night, a quarter string colored lights across the window, and some hang a wreath or put an electric candle in the window. The day the tree goes up is an evening of decorating; there's Christmas Eve dinner with family, and presents at dawn on Christmas morning for households with kids. A few households light a menorah each night of Hanukkah (dates from [Hebcal](https://www.hebcal.com/holidays/chanukah), through 2031). New Year's Eve has more parties. Office towers with a lit crown switch to orange and purple for the last two weeks of October and red and green from December 1 to January 6.
- **Scenes, not loops.** Every activity is a generated script of small beats: dinner burns and turns into a pizza order, someone cries at a movie, fights the TV antenna, dozes off on the sofa, gets up for water at 3 AM, sings into a hairbrush, hits a wrong note on guitar.
- **Shared stories.** Households have events: date nights with candles and slow dancing, arguments (sometimes one of them sleeps on the couch), curtains drawn for the night, parties with guests arriving in waves, birthdays with cake, bedtime stories, kids' nightmares, visitors, pizza delivery, game nights.
- **Display modes.** The controls window switches between four period graphics cards. **VGA** is the normal look. **SVGA** draws close-ups at three times the detail: faces with expressions, shaded furniture, individual book spines, little landscapes in the picture frames and a finer TV picture. **EGA** redraws the screen in the classic 16-color palette, and **CGA** squeezes it into four colors, both with ordered dithering; click CGA again to switch between the cyan/magenta/white palette and the green/red/yellow one. Press `G` to cycle.
- **Cyberpunk mode.** It's hidden: rest the pointer on the sun or the moon (or press and hold on it) until the ring closes, or double-tap it (do it again to switch back; the city always opens in its normal look). The same city goes loud. Outside: a purple smog sky raked by searchlights, cyan and magenta window light, giant megacorp wordmarks running down the sides of towers (OMNICORE, KUROGANE, MONOLITH and friends, each with its own logo, with the odd letter flickering out), garish animated billboards across the facades (scrolling glyphs, a blinking eye, a skull, a noodle bowl), Hong Kong-style vertical banners, capsule pods bolted onto walls, sky bridges and sagging cables between buildings, rooftop holo-billboards, flying cars, rain, steam from the vents and neon reflected on the wet street. Two or three mega towers of 90-plus floors rise behind it all, named for their corporations; they're real buildings, so zoom in and every lit window is an apartment with people in it. The old buildings and the people in them stay exactly as they are (it's the city they live in that changed); the wild stuff happens inside the mega towers: raves (several a night, with lasers, strobes and fog), purple grow rooms, crypto rigs, walls of CRT monitors, jellyfish tanks, robot butlers patrolling the floor, holograms dancing in the living room and stacked capsule beds. Mega tower residents get neon hair, mohawks, glowing visors, gas masks and neon tattoos, and a few are androids; pets may be holograms or robots; and everyday life gets translated ("printing nutrient paste," "on a neural call," "herding the cleaning drone," "at the rave"). Everyone is the same person underneath; turn it off and the city is back to its calm self.
- **Get to know them.** Click a window for each resident's bio, today's mood, what they're doing and what's next, plus a "Lately" journal of the evening.
- **Follow people.** Tick "Follow this apartment" to add a household to your Following list (the camera also stays on them while their window is open). Open **Following** (or press `C`) to check on everyone at once: what each person is doing and how they're feeling, and what has happened since you last looked in (a new job, a breakup, a baby, a move). Click a household to fly there, or use **Visit next** (`N`) to go through them in turn. If the people you followed move out, the list says so and offers to follow the new tenants. The list is saved with your city.

## Controls

Scroll or pinch to zoom, drag to pan, click a window. On the ground floor, click a shop to see who's inside and when it closes, or a lobby for the building's residents, recent move-ins and move-outs, or an office tower's directory. Keys: `Space` pause, `1`–`5` speed, `P` peek into a random window, `S` screensaver (autopilot), `F` follow, `C` following list, `N` visit next followed, `G` display mode, `W` HomeBlocks, `Esc` close / zoom out, `+`/`-` zoom.

**Screensaver button.** Hands the camera to an autopilot. About half the time it holds the classic full skyline, still, the way the old screensaver looked. The rest of the time it takes shots of lively apartments (a party, a date night, someone up at 3 AM): a quick approach and a lingering close-up with a caption, a long slow push-in from the skyline, a drift across facades at mid distance, a crane shot up a building to the water tower, or a tracking shot gliding along a floor from apartment to apartment. Then it slowly pulls back out. Move the mouse or press a key to take over again.

**City code.** The controls window shows a code like `bod5nr-g09`. Open the page with `#city-bod5nr-g09` on the end of the address, or paste the code into the screen saver's options, to watch the same city somewhere else.

## HomeBlocks

Every adult in the city (and every kid 8 and up) has a homepage, the way people did around 1998. Click **Home page** next to a resident's name, or press **HomeBlocks** (`W`) to open the portal in NetSurfer, a little browser window with Back, Forward, Home and a Location bar.

- **Neighborhoods.** Each page lands in the neighborhood that fit the person when they made it: night-shift workers in Graveyard Shift, the newly bereaved in Serenity, cat people in PawPrint Park, marathon trainees in the Colosseum, Halloween fanatics in Spooky Hollow. There are 22, each with its own suburbs and addresses like `/Heartland/Porch/4412/`.
- **In their own words.** Pages are written from each person's real state in the simulation: name, age, job, traits, interests, who they live with, their pets, their current storyline and their household history. A loner writes in lowercase, a sociable person in capitals with too many exclamation points, a retiree thanks the grandchild who set the page up, and a messy person makes typos.
- **All kinds of sites.** Most people made an About Me page, but many built a shrine to a hobby (guitar tab archives, book reviews, a carnivorous plant society of one), a family page with a holiday letter written from what actually happened in their household that year, an online diary whose entries follow their real mood day by day, an ad for a side business, a fan page for a band or TV show that doesn't exist, or a list of links. Some put up a title and an Under Construction sign and never came back. Some have a splash page with a big ENTER link.
- **Subpages** follow their lives: a memorial page for someone they lost, a novel with an excerpt and a word count, a marathon training log, a resume while they're out of work, poems after a breakup, a baby countdown, a night-shift diary, the time right now in the hometown they miss, a research page with optical illusions, a pet's own page, recipes, high scores, a Halloween countdown.
- **The 90s bits:** tiled backgrounds, animated pixel GIFs, Under Construction signs, marquees, blinking text, hit counters, awards, a MIDI player that plays a tune written for that page, sparkle cursor trails, and webrings that link everyone who shares an interest.
- **Guestbooks** are signed by their neighbors and ring-mates, each in their own voice. You can sign them too; your entries are saved in this browser.
- **Kevin's band.** Whoever the city's Kevin is, his homepage is always the official site of POSITRON, from Rochester, New York.
- **Word gets around.** Some residents have found a copy of megaZINE or dial into the MEGAzine BBS, and say so; there's a megaZINE Readers webring. How many grows with megaZINE's real readership (from megazine.org's public visit counts), never fewer than 7% for megaZINE and 5% for the BBS. Those links open the real sites in a new tab.
- **Sam's place.** Every city has the Lings: Sam, Emma, Emmy, Maya and Everett, in a family apartment. Sam is a vision scientist and the SysOp of the MEGAzine BBS, and loves synths and gardening. He has two homepages: The Gabriel Knight Home Page, moved over from his old school account, and the Ling Lab (visual neuroscience), with a contrast-sensitivity chart, a drifting grating that shows motion adaptation, and the lab's people and papers. (Emma and the kids don't have homepages.)
- **Matt's place.** Every city also has Matt Crump, who lives with his wife Jenn and their two cats. His homepage is crumplab, the Computational Cognition Lab: research in hexagons, people, papers, software, and a Stroop test you can take. Both lab sites link out to the real ones.
- **Pages keep a history.** A page's address, neighborhood, look and kind are set when its owner puts it up, a few weeks to months after moving in, and stay put. After that it changes only when they update it: life events become dated entries in a **What's New** box ("Baby Rosie is here!!!", "Posted my resume."), and in between people tinker at their own pace. Loud people and kids update all the time, quiet people and retirees rarely, new parents go silent for a while, and about one in five stop updating altogether, so their page still says "17 days to go!" long after the race. Old sections linger: the marathon log stays up after the marathon, the novel page after the novel. About a third of Under Construction pages eventually get finished. Kids who started a page at 8 redesign it at 13.
- **Family photos.** Pixel snapshots drawn from each person's own look in the city (skin, hair, clothes, hairstyle, glasses, beards). Many front pages have a "This is me!" picture, family pages have real portraits and a latest family photo, and lots of people keep a photo album built from their household's own history: moving day, the new baby, the new cat, graduations, retirements, marathon finishes, then birthdays, school pictures, Halloween costumes, the holidays and summer trips. Click a thumbnail for the big version. They look like drugstore prints: flash, red eye, faded color, film grain, the orange date stamp, the odd blink or thumb over the lens. A few night owls run a WebCam page that shows what they're doing in the city right now.
- **Political obsessives.** About one in twenty residents can't stop blogging about politics. Every city has its own mayor, council and a news cycle of local fights that changes month to month (a stadium bond, a fare hike, bike lanes, a luxury tower, term limits, the noise ordinance...), and taxpayer watchdogs, progressives, libertarians, greens, independents and old-timers all post about the same fight from their own corners. There's also a man who has been tracking one pothole for over a year, and a woman who is just asking questions about the pigeons. With an Election Day countdown, poll results, a Where I Stand table, letters to the editor that never got printed, and guestbooks full of arguments. All the politicians are made up.
- **Amateur news desks.** About one in thirty residents runs a news site: real headlines from the wire, each followed by the resident's own take through their own life (the stargazer heads for the fire escape with binoculars, the retiree remembers when news came in the paper, the marathoner wants to go for a run). The headlines are summaries from [Wikipedia's Current Events portal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portal:Current_events) (CC BY-SA 4.0), which a GitHub workflow (`.github/workflows/news.yml`, `tools/news.py`) fetches into `news.json` every six hours. Only arts, business, environment, science and sports stories get in, and anything about deaths, violence, disasters, crime, courts, war or elections is left out; residents only talk about themselves, never about the real people in the story. Offline (or in the screen saver) the desks say their news feed is down.
- **Novels in progress.** Some residents are writing a book and post it a chapter at a time: people in the middle of a "writing a novel" storyline, working novelists, some readers and word people, and the odd kid. Each book is generated for its author: a genre that suits them (mystery, romance, science fiction, fantasy, horror, literary, thriller, or a kid's adventure), a cast, a setting, a premise, a cover and a back-cover blurb. The chapters themselves are generated prose. New chapters show up in What's New, and most writers stall partway, so the table of contents ends in "Chapter 7: coming soon! I promise."
- **E-mail this page to a friend!** Every page has the link. It opens a form that hands the page to your e-mail program (or the Share sheet on a phone), or lets you copy the link. The link opens the standalone site on the same city and the same day in that city, so your friend sees the page exactly as you did, even if your city has been running fast.
- **Find Window** flies the camera to the person whose page you're reading.
- **In a real browser.** HomeBlocks also runs on its own at [samuelling.github.io/Big-Apple-Sam/web/](https://samuelling.github.io/Big-Apple-Sam/web/). Every page has its own address, like `web/#bod5nr-g09/Heartland/Porch/4412/`, so you can bookmark it, use Back and Forward, or send a link; the code at the front says which city, and an optional `~<day>` after it (`#bod5nr-g09~21150/...`) pins the day in that city without touching the city saved in your own browser. Without a code it shows the city saved in your browser. **New Window** in NetSurfer opens the page you're on there, and guestbook entries you sign show up in both places (they're saved in your browser, so a friend you send a page to won't see them). Each resident's page there has **See my window in the city**, which opens Big Apple Sam at their apartment (`#city-<code>/apt-<n>`); from there, their **Home page** link takes you back.

Pages change as lives change, a new city gets a whole new web, and the people in the cyberpunk mega towers aren't on it.

## Screen savers (macOS and Windows)

The same page also ships as a real screen saver. It runs the autopilot full screen on every display (each display follows a different part of the city), entirely offline: the page is bundled inside, and the savers never go online. GitHub Actions builds both on every push and attaches them to the [latest release](../../releases/tag/latest), with SHA-256 checksums.

**macOS 12 or later.** Open `Big-Apple-Sam-Mac.dmg`, double-click `Big Apple Sam.saver`, and choose to install it for this user. It appears under **System Settings → Screen Saver**. Click **Options…** to paste a city code, pick the clock speed (live by default), pick CGA, EGA, VGA or SVGA, and turn "Always night", cyberpunk mode or the captions on and off.
Once the build is signed with a Developer ID and notarized by Apple (see [SIGNING.md](SIGNING.md)), that's all there is to it. Until then, macOS refuses the first time: open **System Settings → Privacy & Security**, scroll down, and click **Open Anyway** next to the Big Apple Sam message.

**Windows 10 and 11.** Run `Big-Apple-Sam-Windows-Setup.exe`. It installs Big Apple Sam into the system's screen saver list and opens Screen Saver Settings with it selected; **Settings…** has the same options as on the Mac. (Or unzip `Big-Apple-Sam-Windows-scr.zip`, right-click `BigAppleSam.scr` and choose **Install**.) It uses Microsoft Edge WebView2, which comes with Windows 10 and 11. It isn't code-signed, so Windows may say "Windows protected your PC": choose **More info**, then **Run anyway**.

**Linux.** There's no common screen saver system to plug into, so use the web version in a full-screen browser and press `S`.

To build them yourself: on a Mac, `brew install xcodegen`, then in `saver/` run `xcodegen generate` and open `BigAppleSam.xcodeproj`. On Windows, from a Developer PowerShell in `windows/`, run `nuget install Microsoft.Web.WebView2 -Version 1.0.4258.31 -OutputDirectory packages`, then `.\build.ps1`.
